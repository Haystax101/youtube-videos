# Competitor context: what successful faceless "what happens if" Shorts do

Researched 2026-11-25. Evidence is mixed: a few numbers are public (Tubefilter charts), most
"formula" claims come from creator blogs and tool vendors, and some are our own viewing notes.
Each point says which. Update this file when the analytics tell us something new.

## The channels worth studying

| Channel | What it is | What to take |
|---|---|---|
| **Zack D. Films** | 3D-animated 30-45 s Shorts: how the body works, survival, how things work, weird true stories. ~1.7-1.9 billion views a *month* in 2024 (Tubefilter US Top 100). Biggest Short: "How Stitches Work", ~253M views, 31 s. | One mechanism per video, shown not told; first frame is already the strange thing (no title card); a new shot every ~2.5-3.5 s; titles of a few plain words + one emoji; descriptions basically empty. Many end on a practical takeaway ("so next time..."), not a death. |
| **What If 3D Science** | One everyday science question per Short ("What happens if you microwave a grape?"), daily. | The question *is* the title and the hook; the viewer stays to check their guess. |
| **Life Noggin** | Animated "What happens to your body if..." / "What would happen if you never showered?" | The "never X / only X / every day X" premise family, explained by mechanism, often with a useful answer at the end. |
| **Institute of Human Anatomy** (our viewing notes) | Real anatomy, health questions ("what smoking does to lungs"). | Health facts land when there's one concrete visual and a number. |
| **MiloQ** (our viewing notes; george's reference) | Illustrated comic history with costumed characters and dialogue. | Real history played as a sitcom: a goal, a funny exchange, an ironic ending. |
| **History Matters / OverSimplified** | Animated history, "Why did...?" framing, jokes. | Cause → consequence → ironic twist. A history story needs a *turn*, not just an event. |
| **The Infographics Show / old Veo Shorts of george's** | Survival what-ifs; george's own "bitten by 10,000 snakes" got 18k views at 95% viewed in 24 s. | A single vivid absurd premise in the title beats a slow build. |

## Patterns that recur (and how sure we are)

1. **The first frame is the premise happening** (strong agreement across sources, and our own data:
   cold opens on bees/sitting beat the rest). No logo, no "DAY 1" setup first.
2. **One question, answered by a mechanism.** Zack D's best videos explain *how* one thing works
   (stitches, a knot). The surprise is the mechanism, not the gore.
3. **Every line opens a small loop.** Each sentence should make the viewer need the next one
   ("...but that's not what kills you"). Vendor/creator advice, consistent with retention curves.
4. **Fast visual change.** New shot every 2.5-3.5 s (measured on Zack D-style channels by a
   third-party guide). For us: change camera or pose every beat, beats of 3-5 s.
5. **Useful payoff.** A large share of Zack D Shorts end with a takeaway the viewer can use
   ("always let a sneeze out"). Useful = shareable. This is george's "impactful and useful" note.
6. **Loop the ending into the start** (anecdotal, but cheap to try): last line or image echoes
   the first, so the replay feels seamless.
7. **Packaging over metadata.** Plain few-word titles naming the premise; hashtags/descriptions
   matter little.
8. **Avoid toilet/poo topics.** Our Groom of the Stool got ~0 views (likely topic + re-upload).
   Gross-out body content works for Zack D only when it's medical, not scatological.

## Our twist (why someone watches us instead)

- **It's a simulation of YOU.** "You" narration plus time labels (DAY 1 → YEAR 2) turns a fact
  video into a story the viewer is living. Zack D explains; we *play it out*.
- **Gary the recurring avatar**, who can die, survive, or come out better. The ending is no longer
  guessable, which is itself a reason to stay to the end.
- **One real, checkable "wait, what?" fact per video**, plus a useful takeaway when there is one.

## What NOT to copy

- Don't recreate a specific channel's look or characters (MiloQ, Zack D). Take structure, not style.
- Don't fake "true story" framing for invented events: history scripts must be real and checkable.
- Avoid templated near-identical uploads (YouTube's 2025 inauthentic-content rule). Each video
  needs its own idea, scenes and jokes.
